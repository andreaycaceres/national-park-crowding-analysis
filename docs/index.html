Introduction

Back in 1864, the Yosemite Grant was passed by President Abraham Lincoln, stating that Yosemite Valley and Mariposa Grove will be maintained for the public's use. (National Park Service, 2022) Then, following presidents passed bills creating more National Parks and allowing presidents to preserve land for educational, recreational, and historical purposes. For example, Theodore Roosevelt signed a law from Congress that gave the president power to create a National Monument on land that is controlled by the government. In August 1916, President Woodrow Wilson signed the Organic Act, legislation to create a new federal bureau responsible for protecting and conserving 35 national parks and monuments (NPS, 2022). Thus, the National Park Service was created. Today, there are over 400 sites and over 84 million acres are cared for by the National Park Service (NPS, 2022). This acreage spans over all 50 states, the Virgin Islands, Puerto Rico, the District of Columbia, American Samoa, and Saipan (NPS, 2022). These areas have been deemed of special recognition and deserve to be protected through legislation. Today, over 20,000 employees work for the National Park Service to preserve these lands and assist the hundreds of millions of people that visit these parks (National Park Service, 2026). In 2025, 323 million people visited these lands with 29% of them visiting national parks, that is approximately 93 million people (NPS, n.d.).

Over the years the interest from the public in these public lands has grown exponentially. Millions of people from all over the world travel to the United States to enjoy the various activities and attractions national parks have to offer. While the peaked interest from the public in getting outdoors is beneficial, this influx in visitation can cause strain on the National Park Service. With the current presidential administration, major budget cuts, forced resignations, and hiring freezes have left the National Park Service to struggle with the limited sources provided. There are questions that remain about what factors lead to overcrowding, which parks are gaining the most attraction in the past ten years, and what activities attract the most visitors. Insights from research into visitation patterns can help allocate where resources need to go and which factors are leading to visitation overcrowding. Research like this will help individuals such as those in the National Park Service, volunteers, stakeholders, and the public who visit these parks all year long. The reason as to why this research matters today is because the "Outdoor Boom" which started during COVID and peaked around 2023-2024. This created a massive surge in lifestyle changes based around nature-involved activities. This trend has left people spending more time outdoors, caring more about conservation, and putting their income towards outdoor recreation. So far, there has been research into visitor satisfaction, recreation management, tourism patterns, and accessibility. Through this research, national parks can become more efficient and further prepared to assist visitors while preserving the beauty and history of these lands.

As visitation continues to increase, understanding how people use national parks becomes increasingly important. Different parks offer different recreational opportunities, landscapes, amenities, and visitor experiences, which can influence the number and type of visitors they attract. There are parks that experience heavy crowding throughout the year, while others are less visited despite offering similar activities and attractions. Examining these differences can provide valuable insight into what characteristics are associated with higher visitation and recreation activity. If we can understand these patterns, we can help park managers better anticipate visitor needs and allocate resources more effectively. Increased visitation can place pressure on trails, campgrounds, visitor centers, and park staff; making logistics and resource management more important than ever. Identifying trends in visitation and recreation can also help determine which parks may require additional support or infrastructure improvements. Also, understanding visitor behavior and patterns can assist in handling public access along with conservation efforts. As national parks continue to grow in popularity, data driven research can provide valuable information for decision makers and stakeholders. Ultimately, studying visitation patterns can help ensure that these public lands remain protected, accessible, and enjoyable for future visitors and park lovers.

To address the research questions below, this project applies a combination of data science and machine learning techniques to a national parks visitation dataset spanning recreation, lodging, camping, location, visitation, and entrance fee characteristics. Various techniques will be used to analyze and draw conclusions from future findings. These findings can help with future national park decision making. The analysis begins with exploratory data analysis and clustering to uncover natural groupings among parks based on shared visitation and recreation patterns. Principal Component Analysis is then used to reduce the complexity of the dataset and identify which features carry the most influence over visitation outcomes with the least information loss. Supervised classification models, including Naive Bayes and Decision Trees, are trained to predict whether a park falls into a Low, Medium, or High visitation category. Each method offers a different lens through which to view the data and together they provide a more complete picture than any single technique could on its own. By comparing the strengths and weaknesses of these models, this project aims to identify which park characteristics are the most reliable for predicting crowding. Ultimately, the goal is to turn these findings into practical insight that park managers and stakeholders can use to better prepare for rising visitation in the years ahead.

National parks vary significantly in their size, geography, accessibility, and recreational activities, making them great candidates for data-driven analysis. Some premier national parks receive millions of visitors each year. In contrast, others remain uncrowded despite offering similar outdoor experiences. These differences suggest that visitation is influenced by more than just a park's popularity or location. Factors such as camping availability, lodging options, recreation opportunities, entrance fees, and region may all contribute to how visitors choose where to travel. By examining these variables together rather than individually it becomes possible to identify patterns that are not obvious through descriptive statistics alone. Machine learning techniques can provide an opportunity to uncover these underlying relationships and determine which park characteristics are most strongly associated with high visitation. Rather than focusing on a single park, this project compares national parks across the United States to better understand how they relate to one another. The findings of this study can help identify parks with similar visitor profiles and determine which factors contribute most to crowding. The analysis evaluates how accurately these visitation categories can be predicted from available data. These insights may support future planning efforts related to staffing, infrastructure improvements, conservation priorities, and visitor management strategies. This research demonstrates how data mining techniques can transform large collections of park data into important information that supports evidence based decision making.

Q&A

1. Which national parks receive the most visitors?

Great Smoky Mountains beat all national parks in recreation visits during the period studied, with Grand Canyon, Yosemite, Yellowstone, Zion, and Rocky Mountain following as the parks with the largest visitor totals. These parks pulled ahead of the rest of the system year after year.

2. Which national parks experienced the largest increases in visitation?

Growth wasn't spread evenly across the park system. National parks that are western and outdoor recreation focused picked up visitors greater than the rest. Although, the exact rate varied by park and by year. Even with that variation, the trend shows an increase in interest across all parks.

3. What seasonal visitation patterns exist?

Summer was the peak for most parks. Summer months are most popular due to favorable weather and families traveling during school breaks. Winter visitation dropped off across the parks, but how much depended on a park's climate and location. Parks with warmer climates or year-round activities had visitors more consistently than colder more seasonal ones.

4. How did COVID-19 affect national park visitation?

Visitation decreased drastically in the early months of the pandemic as travel restrictions and closures took place. Visitation increased again once people started looking for outdoor, socially-distanced activities. This shift, the Outdoor Boom, caused an increased interest in hiking, camping, and outdoor recreation even higher than before.

5. Are Pacific Northwest national parks becoming more crowded?

Not evenly. Olympic and Mount Rainier consistently pulled in more visitors than Crater Lake and North Cascades even though all four are in the same region. That gap in visitation sheds light on factors like location, accessibility and existing visitor base still causing a difference within a region.

6. What park characteristics are associated with higher crowding?

No single factor drives crowding on its own. Camping activity, lodging, backcountry use, recreation opportunities, and the number of available activities all played a role in separating heavily visited parks from less popular ones.

7. Which parks appear most vulnerable to overcrowding?

Great Smoky Mountains, Yosemite, Grand Canyon, Yellowstone, and Zion top the list. These parks combine high visitor numbers with heavy demand on trails, lodging, camping, and transportation: a combination that puts strain on infrastructure, natural resources, and park staff.

8. Can parks be accurately classified by crowding level?

Yes to a certain point. The models classified parks into crowding categories with moderate success. Certain approaches handled it better than others and several struggled to separate high-crowding parks from low-crowding parks. Crowding can be estimated from park characteristics, but more data and additional features would better the results.

9. How do visitation patterns differ by region?

Regions do matter when it comes to visitation patterns. Alaska is lowest for both visits-per-park and total-visits by region, most parks stay under a few thousand visits and the region never breaks 5M total visits in a year. Northeast has the highest per-park median at around 110,000 visits, but its total visits are lower than several other regions. Northeast visitation peaks around 12-13M in July. Intermountain has the highest total visits by far, peaking around 63M in July. This seems to be because it has more parks or a few very large ones pulling the total up. Pacific West is second in total visits, peaking near 30M. Midwest and Southeast are close to each other in total visits, both peaking around 20M. Southeast has a lower per-park median than Northeast but holds the single highest outlier of any region, the Great Smoky Mountains.

10. What role do recreational activities play in visitation and crowding?

Visitors choose parks largely based on what they can do there: hike, camp, view scenery, watch wildlife, or find backcountry recreation and lodging. No single activity fully explains crowding on its own; the busiest parks tend to offer several of these experiences together rather than excelling at just one. The park with the most recreational activities offered is the Great Sand Dunes National Park in Colorado. Although this park offered the most activities, it did not break the top ten most visited national parks. This could be due to lack of outreach, accessibility issues, etc. We cannot confirm that the more activities there are, the more visitation.

Unanswered Question - Can future visitation be predicted?

Predicting future visitation is the one question this project couldn't fully answer. The analysis focused on understanding past patterns and classifying current crowding levels rather than forecasting. A real forecast would need a time-series approach plus data on future weather, closures, economic conditions, and travel behavior that fell outside this project's scope.
